Riddle games for classes, parties and family evenings

A single riddle is a pleasant thirty seconds. Ten riddles with teams, turns and a score become a game that can hold a class, a party or a whole family for half an hour. Here are six formats, from the quietest to the loudest. All of them work with the riddle sets on this site, in Hindi or in English.

1. Riddle relay (for a classroom)

Divide the class into rows or teams. Read a riddle to the first team. If the team answers within twenty seconds, it scores two points. If not, the riddle passes to the next team for one point. Keep the riddles moving quickly. The passing rule matters: it gives every team a reason to listen to every riddle, not only its own.

2. Chit bowl (for a party)

Write riddles on chits and fold them into a bowl. Each guest draws a chit and reads the riddle aloud to the room. If the reader can also answer it, they keep the chit as a point; if not, anyone may answer and win it. The guest with the most chits at the end wins. Shy guests like this format, because reading aloud is easy and nobody is put on the spot.

3. Riddle of the day (for a home or a class board)

Write one riddle on a board, on the fridge or in the family group each morning, and reveal the answer at dinner or at the last bell. Nobody answers aloud during the day; each person tells the quizmaster privately. A week of this builds a small daily habit that children look forward to. The home page of this site shows a fresh pair of riddles every day for the purpose.

4. Treasure hunt with riddles (for children)

Use riddles whose answers are things in the house: a clock, a mirror, a broom, the fridge. Hide the next riddle at the place the answer names, and a small prize at the end. The Hindi set घर की चीज़ें and the English What am I? set are written about household things and suit this well. Keep clues away from stoves, balconies and high shelves.

5. WhatsApp riddle hour (for a chat group)

Announce a time. Post the rules, then one riddle every five minutes. The first right answer gets the point, and the quizmaster's reading of the clock is final. For a big group, ask members to send answers privately and post the scores after every riddle, so that slower typists have a chance. Each set page has one button that sends the whole set, and another that copies all the answers for the quizmaster.

6. Make your own (for older children and adults)

Each player gets five minutes to write a riddle about an object in the room. Riddles are read out, and the room guesses. A point goes to the writer for every guesser who is fooled, and a bonus to the riddle voted the cleverest. It is the best format of all for learning how riddles work; our page on how to solve riddles shows the tricks that writers use.

Tips for the quizmaster

  • Read each riddle twice, slowly, and pause before taking answers.
  • Match the difficulty to the youngest player. Begin with easy ones; early success keeps everyone in the game.
  • Accept any answer that truly fits the clues, even if it is not the one on the page.
  • Give hints in a fixed order: first the kind of thing, then where it is found, then the first letter.
  • Praise good wrong answers. A clever miss shows more thinking than a lucky hit.
  • Stop while people are asking for one more. Ten or twelve riddles is a full round.

Choosing the sets

For small children, use the easy riddles and animal riddles. For mixed family groups, the food and nature sets suit every age. For adults and teenagers, bring out the tricky riddles and the maths riddles. Or let the site choose: play a random round of ten.
